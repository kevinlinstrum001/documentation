# 1. Program Overview

## What PST-SP Is

Problem-Solving Therapy for Suicide Prevention, or **PST-SP**, is a structured
treatment program designed to help Veterans manage stressful problems,
emotional distress, and suicidal thinking by strengthening practical coping and
problem-solving skills.

The program treats problem solving as a learnable process. A person may already
possess some useful problem-solving abilities but have difficulty accessing or
applying them when overwhelmed, discouraged, emotionally activated, or under
significant stress.

## Central Premise

Stressful life problems do not automatically produce one fixed emotional
outcome. The way a person understands, approaches, and responds to a problem can
influence whether the situation leads toward adaptation or increasing distress.

PST-SP therefore focuses on improving the process between:

- encountering a stressful problem;
- interpreting and organizing the situation;
- regulating immediate emotional reactions;
- generating possible responses;
- selecting and carrying out a plan;
- reviewing whether the response helped.

## Skills Taught

The introductory material identifies a practical sequence of skills:

- defining problems;
- brainstorming possible solutions;
- making decisions;
- trying selected solutions;
- monitoring how well those solutions worked.

This makes PST-SP both a therapeutic method and a repeatable operational
workflow.

# 2. The PST-SP Treatment Structure

The packet is organized as a progression of toolkits. The sequence is important:
the program does not immediately demand analytical problem solving from someone
whose emotions and attention are overloaded.

## Toolkit Sequence

1. **Introduction and Rationale**
2. **Stop and Slow Down**
3. **Visualization for Hope and Motivation**
4. **Overcoming Brain Overload**
5. **Planful Problem Solving**
6. **Review and Future Forecasting**
7. **Optional Handouts**
8. **Measures**

## Dependency Logic

The structure suggests the following dependency chain:

1. Recognize distress and interrupt escalation.
2. Restore enough calm to think deliberately.
3. reconnect with hope, purpose, or motivation.
4. reduce cognitive overload.
5. apply a structured problem-solving method.
6. review the result and prepare for future problems.

In systems terms, the program first stabilizes the operating condition before
asking the person to perform higher-order planning.

# 3. The Problem-Solving Model of Stress

## Basic Model

The introductory material presents problem solving as the process that helps
determine how a person responds to stressful life events and chronic daily
problems.

A simplified interpretation is:

```text
Stressful problem
       |
       v
Problem-solving response
       |
       +--> Effective response --> More positive adjustment
       |
       +--> Ineffective response --> Greater emotional distress
                                      and possible suicidal thinking
```

The model does not imply that distress is caused by personal failure. Instead,
it identifies a process that can be examined, supported, and improved.

## Effective Problem Solving

Effective problem solving generally involves:

- slowing down enough to understand the situation;
- describing the problem in specific terms;
- identifying what can and cannot be changed;
- generating more than one possible response;
- choosing a realistic action;
- testing the action;
- observing the result;
- revising the plan when necessary.

## Ineffective Problem Solving

Problem solving may become less effective when a person:

- feels emotionally overwhelmed;
- sees the situation as impossible or permanent;
- reacts impulsively;
- avoids the problem;
- becomes cognitively overloaded;
- cannot identify a manageable next step;
- judges the entire process as a failure after one poor result.

# 4. Obstacles to Effective Problem Solving

## The A-to-B Model

The packet frames problem solving as movement from a present condition, **A**,
to a desired condition, **B**. The central question is not only “What is the
problem?” but also “What is blocking movement toward the desired state?”

**Physical description:**

The visual distinguishes the original life problem from the barriers that

## Obstacle-to-Toolkit Mapping

| Obstacle | Matching Toolkit |
|---|---|
| Brain freeze or brain overload | Overcoming Brain Overload |
| Strong negative emotions | Stop and Slow Down |
| Hopelessness | Visualization for Hope and Motivation |
| Ineffective problem solving | Planful Problem Solving |

This mapping is the basic architecture of PST-SP: identify the dominant obstacle,
select the matching toolkit, restore capacity, and then proceed.

## Emotional Overload

Strong emotions can narrow attention and make it difficult to distinguish the
problem itself from the emotional reaction to it.

## Hopelessness

When a person cannot imagine improvement, generating or testing alternatives may
feel pointless. The program therefore treats hope and motivation as active
problem-solving resources.

## Brain Overload

Cognitive overload can interfere with memory, organization, sequencing,
decision-making, and follow-through. A complex problem may need to be externalized
and divided into smaller parts before it can be addressed.

## Avoidance

Avoidance may offer temporary relief while allowing the underlying problem,
uncertainty, or consequences to grow.

## Impulsive Action

Acting before defining the problem can produce a response to the strongest
feeling rather than to the actual situation.

## Pessimistic Problem Beliefs

Beliefs such as “nothing will work,” “I should already know how to fix this,” or
“having this problem means I have failed” can interfere with deliberate action.

# 5. Toolkit One: Stop and Slow Down

## Purpose

This toolkit is used when anger, fear, anxiety, or other strong emotions are
causing substantial distress or interfering with the ability to solve problems.

Its objectives are to:

- recognize triggers and early signs of escalation;
- distinguish rising distress from an active crisis;
- use regulation strategies before cognitive capacity is lost;
- calm the brain and body;
- return to a condition in which deliberate problem solving is possible.

## Toolkit Handouts

The source packet includes:

- Stop and Slow Down versus Safety Planning;
- Personal Triggers Worksheet;
- Slow Down Strategies;
- Safe-Place Visualization;
- Deep Breathing;
- Intentional or “Fake” Yawning;
- Mindful Meditation;
- Deep Muscle Relaxation;
- Mindful Walking.

## Recognizing Triggers

A trigger is an external event that may produce internal reactions. The packet
distinguishes the event itself from the resulting emotions, thoughts, bodily
sensations, memories, and urges.

### Trigger Categories

- interpersonal events;
- physical conditions such as noise, climate, proximity, or crowds;
- other external events;
- emotional reactions;
- thoughts, memories, or mental images;
- physical sensations;
- urges to act differently.

This distinction supports early detection. The aim is to notice the chain near
its beginning rather than only after the reaction has become overwhelming.

## Selecting Target Problems

The participant identifies two or three problems that:

- contribute to suicidal thoughts or wanting to die;
- can be worked on during the program;
- are sufficiently specific to support practical action.

The purpose is not to solve every difficulty at once, but to define a manageable
treatment scope.

## Slow Down Strategy Menu

| Strategy | Function | Typical Duration |
|---|---|---:|
| Safe-place visualization | Use relaxing imagery to create a mentally safe setting | 10–15 minutes |
| Deep breathing | Reduce physiological arousal through diaphragmatic breathing | 1–5 minutes |
| Intentional yawning | Produce a rapid calming and focusing response | 10–30 seconds or several yawns |
| Mindful meditation | Notice and release distressing thoughts and feelings | 10–40 minutes |
| Deep muscle relaxation | Reduce physical tension throughout the body | 25–30 minutes |
| Mindful walking | Use movement and present-moment awareness | 20–40 minutes |

The source recommends selecting one or two strategies for focused practice and
testing others between sessions.

## Exercise Collection

### Exercise: Safe-Place Visualization

**Source basis:**  
PST-SP handout “Go on a Vacation in Your Mind: Visualize to Reduce Stress,”
attributed in the packet to Nezu et al. (2013).

**Purpose:**  
Reduce emotional arousal, increase a sense of safety, and create enough mental
space to slow down before problem solving.

**When to use:**  

- during rising distress, anxiety, or anger;
- when cognitive overload is beginning;
- before a difficult anticipated event;
- when falling asleep is difficult;
- before the situation has reached crisis.

**Estimated time:**  
10–15 minutes.

**Preparation:**  

- choose a comfortable, quiet location;
- reduce distractions and lower the lighting if useful;
- loosen restrictive clothing;
- consider recording the script so it can be followed without memorization;
- practice regularly rather than waiting for severe distress.

**Core method:**  
Imagine a place that feels peaceful, private, and safe. Build it through sight,
sound, touch, smell, temperature, distance, texture, and movement. The place may
be real, remembered, invented, indoors, or outdoors. Another person may be
included only if desired.

**Exercise sequence:**

1. Close the eyes gently and slow the breath.
2. Imagine entering a personally meaningful safe place.
3. Observe the scene at a distance and close up.
4. Add sensory detail: sights, sounds, smells, textures, temperature, and ground
   beneath the feet.
5. Adjust the environment until it feels comfortable and secure.
6. Notice the body becoming calmer.
7. Repeat a simple statement such as, “I feel relaxed and calm.”
8. Remain in the scene for as long as useful.
9. Leave the scene slowly by the same imagined path.
10. Reorient to the room and carry the calmer state back into the present.

**Practice guidance:**  
The exercise is presented as a trainable skill. Daily practice for at least one
week is recommended in the source handout so that the imagery becomes easier to
access during stress.

### Exercise: Deep Diaphragmatic Breathing

**Purpose:**  
Use slow abdominal breathing to reduce physiological arousal, interrupt
escalating stress, and prepare for clearer thinking.

**When to use:**  

- anxiety or anger;
- feeling overwhelmed;
- before difficult conversations;
- before deliberate problem solving;
- before sleep;
- during ordinary public situations after the skill has been practiced.

**Estimated time:**  
3–5 minutes.

**Preparation:**  
Sit or lie comfortably. Initially, place one hand on the upper chest and the
other on the abdomen below the rib cage.

**Core method:**

1. Notice which hand moves most during ordinary breathing.
2. Inhale slowly into the abdomen so the lower hand rises more than the chest
   hand.
3. Imagine the abdomen filling like a balloon.
4. Exhale slowly through gently pursed lips.
5. Allow the abdomen to fall as the breath leaves.
6. Continue for approximately five minutes.
7. Optionally pair the breath with a short calming phrase.

**Practice guidance:**  
Daily practice is encouraged so the method can eventually be used discreetly
without closing the eyes or using hand placement.

### Exercise: Intentional or “Fake” Yawning

**Purpose:**  
Use repeated intentional yawning as a rapid regulation method intended to reduce
tension and improve concentration.

**When to use:**  

- before demanding tasks;
- during rising stress;
- before public speaking or performance;
- during mental fatigue;
- when a very brief intervention is needed.

**Estimated time:**  
Approximately 30 seconds to 2 minutes.

**Core method:**

1. Deliberately imitate a full yawn.
2. Repeat the motion several times.
3. Continue until spontaneous yawning begins or the body feels less tense.
4. Observe any change in alertness, breathing, or concentration.

**Practice guidance:**  
The source suggests intentionally yawning several times, noting that natural
yawns may begin after repeated imitation. The exercise is presented as quick,
free, and usable in many settings.

# 8. Toolkit Four: Planful Problem Solving

## Purpose

Planful problem solving is the deliberate workflow used after sufficient
stabilization, motivation, and cognitive organization have been established.

## Preliminary Workflow

### 1. Define the Problem

Describe what is happening in concrete, specific, and observable terms.

### 2. Identify the Desired Outcome

State what improvement would look like. The goal should be realistic and
sufficiently specific to evaluate.

### 3. Identify Obstacles

Determine what is preventing movement from the current condition to the desired
condition.

### 4. Generate Alternatives

Produce several possible responses before evaluating or rejecting them.

### 5. Compare Alternatives

Consider feasibility, effort, risk, consequences, resources, and likely short-
and long-term effects.

### 6. Select a Response

Choose the most workable option or combination of options.

### 7. Make an Action Plan

Specify:

- the next action;
- who is involved;
- when it will occur;
- what resources are required;
- likely barriers;
- a backup action.

### 8. Implement the Plan

Carry out the selected action in manageable steps.

### 9. Monitor the Result

Observe what changed and compare the result with the desired outcome.

### 10. Revise or Repeat

Retain what helped, change what did not, and repeat the process when necessary.

# 9. Problem-Solving Beliefs and Styles

## Positive Problem-Solving Beliefs

The packet describes a constructive stance as **realistic optimism**. This is not
the claim that every outcome will be favorable. It is the belief that problems
are often manageable and that deliberate effort can improve many situations.

A realistic optimist generally believes that:

- problems are not automatically unfixable disasters;
- negative emotions can provide useful information;
- many life problems are manageable;
- effort can improve a situation, even when it cannot make it perfect;
- difficult problems require persistence.

## Negative Problem-Solving Beliefs

A pessimistic orientation tends to:

- treat problems as major threats to well-being;
- doubt personal coping ability;
- become overwhelmed by distress;
- predict that efforts will fail;
- expect negative outcomes from other people.

This distinction separates **beliefs about problems** from **technical
problem-solving skill**. A person may understand the steps but still be unable
to use them if the underlying orientation says that action is pointless.

## Problem-Solving Styles

### Planful Style — “The Thoughtful Planner”

A deliberate style that defines the problem, considers alternatives, chooses a
course of action, and reviews the result.

### Avoidant Style — “The Procrastinator”

A style characterized by delay, withdrawal, or waiting for the problem to solve
itself.

### Impulsive Style — “The Quick Fixer”

A style characterized by rapid action before the problem and consequences have
been adequately considered.

content is preserved more clearly as text.

# Mindful Meditation

## Purpose

Develop the ability to observe thoughts and emotions without treating them as
facts, commands, or definitions of identity.

## Core Principle

The participant practices becoming an independent observer:

> I am having a thought; I am not the thought.

## Method

1. Sit comfortably with eyes open or closed.
2. Feel the natural breath without trying to control it.
3. Notice the inhale and exhale.
4. When attention wanders, return gently to breathing.
5. Observe thoughts without judgment.
6. Imagine thoughts floating away like leaves on a stream.
7. Notice bodily sensations and environmental sounds.
8. Do not suppress experience; observe and release it.
9. Return repeatedly to the present moment.

# Progressive Muscle Relaxation

## Purpose

Reduce physical tension and interrupt the feedback loop in which anxiety creates
muscle tightness and muscle tightness signals further anxiety.

## Session Length

Approximately 25–30 minutes.

## Basic Cycle

1. Focus on one muscle group.
2. Tense for several seconds.
3. Notice the tension.
4. Release fully.
5. Notice the contrast.
6. Continue through the body.

## Muscle Sequence

- hands and forearms;
- biceps;
- forehead and scalp;
- eyes, jaw, tongue, and lips;
- neck and shoulders;
- chest and abdomen;
- lower back;
- buttocks and thighs;
- calves, shins, feet, and toes.

# Mindful Wabi-Sabi Walk

## Purpose

Practice mindfulness through slow walking, breathing, sensory attention, and
appreciation of imperfection, impermanence, and incompleteness.

## Method

1. Begin with mindful breathing.
2. Stay with the present.
3. Notice each footstep.
4. Coordinate breathing with several steps when useful.
5. Observe sounds, smells, surfaces, people, plants, and weather.
6. Notice age, wear, asymmetry, and change.
7. Allow thoughts to pass.
8. Consider what may be present but not visible.
9. Carry greater awareness into the rest of the day.

# Optional Handout 1: Becoming Emotionally Aware

## Developing Emotional Mindfulness

### Purpose

This handout expands the Stop and Slow Down toolkit by teaching the participant
to identify, name, observe, and interpret emotional reactions before acting on
them.

Its central premise is:

> Emotions are not merely states to suppress. They are signals that can provide
> information about what is happening, what matters, and what may require action.

The handout emphasizes that naming an emotion and observing it deliberately can
reduce its immediate intensity and increase the person’s ability to choose a
response.

## Six-Step Process

### Step 1: Name the Emotion

When distress or physical discomfort begins:

- stop;
- identify the first emotion that appears;
- rate its intensity;
- put the feeling into words;
- write it down when possible.

Externalizing the emotion can improve recall and clarify the experience.

### Step 2: Observe How the Emotion Appears

Notice:

- physical sensations;
- changes in breathing;
- heart rate;
- tension;
- heat or flushing;
- throat or stomach sensations;
- self-talk;
- mood changes;
- urges;
- behavioral changes.

These are treated as signals that something requires attention.

### Step 3: Stop, Be Aware, and Slow Down

Use an imagined stop sign, flashing red light, or pause button.

For several seconds:

- stop acting;
- stop speaking when possible;
- allow the emotion to be present;
- identify it before trying to eliminate it;
- avoid denying or immediately escaping the feeling.

### Step 4: Combine Emotion and Logic

Emotion without reflection may lead to impulsive action. Logic without emotional
awareness may ignore important information.

The handout defines wisdom as integrating both:

```text
Emotional information + deliberate reasoning = wiser action
```

### Step 5: Ask What the Emotion Is Telling You

The emotional signal may point toward:

- a situation that needs to change;
- an inaccurate or exaggerated self-statement;
- a loss;
- a threat;
- an unmet need;
- a reality that must be accepted;
- a problem that requires planning.

### Step 6: Decide What Can Change and What Requires Acceptance

Work toward changing what can realistically be changed. Use acceptance and more
realistic thinking for what cannot be changed.

Once calmer, proceed to Planful Problem Solving.

## Integrated Workflow

```text
Notice activation
      |
      v
Name the emotion
      |
      v
Observe body, thoughts, urges, and behavior
      |
      v
Pause
      |
      v
Interpret the emotional information
      |
      v
Combine emotion with logic
      |
      v
Choose acceptance, change, or problem solving
```

No source image is required for this handout. A later original visual could show
emotion as an instrument panel or signal system, but the concept is adequately
preserved in text.

# Optional Handout 2: Listening to Your Feelings

## Purpose

This handout provides a structured interpretation guide for several common
negative emotions.

For each emotion, it asks:

1. What information should I look for?
2. What question should I ask myself?
3. Why does this information matter?
4. What type of response may be appropriate?

The guide does not treat emotions as infallible. Emotional signals may reflect
real conditions, distorted beliefs, or both. They must be examined rather than
obeyed automatically.

## Fear or Anxiety

### Information to Look For

Possible threat, pain, rejection, humiliation, failure, job loss, physical harm,
or other anticipated danger.

Possible signs include:

- nervous thoughts;
- sweating;
- dry mouth;
- stomach distress;
- rapid heartbeat;
- dizziness;
- shallow breathing;
- urges to flee, hide, or avoid.

### Question to Ask

> What am I afraid will happen?

### Why It Matters

The answer can help distinguish realistic danger from exaggerated prediction and
support a more deliberate response than avoidance alone.

## Anger

### Information to Look For

A sense of being blocked from something wanted or valued. The obstacle may be a
person, circumstance, rule, limitation, or expectation.

### Question to Ask

> What do I want, and what is blocking it?

### Why It Matters

Anger may reveal:

- an unmet goal;
- wounded pride;
- a perceived injustice;
- unrealistic expectations;
- a need to redirect effort toward a more useful action.

## Sadness

### Information to Look For

Actual or perceived loss of:

- a person;
- relationship;
- role;
- status;
- ability;
- power;
- valued activity;
- sense of importance.

### Question to Ask

> What do I believe I have lost?

### Why It Matters

Clarifying the loss can support grief, restoration of meaningful activity, and a
more accurate separation between personal worth and what was lost.

## Embarrassment

### Information to Look For

Vulnerability and concern that others can see mistakes, imperfections,
limitations, or emotions.

### Question to Ask

> What am I afraid other people can see or conclude about me?

### Why It Matters

The answer may point toward harsh self-judgment, excessive dependence on others’
approval, or difficulty accepting ordinary imperfection.

## Guilt

### Information to Look For

Regret concerning something done, omitted, or believed to have harmed another
person.

### Question to Ask

> What do I regret, and is repair or personal change needed?

### Why It Matters

Guilt may support:

- acknowledgment;
- apology;
- restitution;
- behavioral change;
- correction of excessive self-blame;
- development of a less approval-dependent inner voice.

## Quick-Reference Matrix

| Emotion | Possible signal | Primary question | Likely next task |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fear / anxiety | Threat or predicted harm | What am I afraid will happen? | Assess realism, safety, and coping options |
| Anger | Blocked goal or perceived injustice | What do I want, and what is blocking it? | Clarify goal, expectations, and action |
| Sadness | Loss | What have I lost? | Grieve, restore meaning, seek support |
| Embarrassment | Exposure or social evaluation | What am I afraid others see? | Examine self-judgment and approval needs |
| Guilt | Regret or perceived wrongdoing | What requires repair or change? | Repair, learn, or correct distorted blame |

# Optional Handout 3: Expanding the Emotion-Regulation Toolkit

## Purpose

This handout adds three broad strategies for reducing stress and strong negative
emotion:

1. Choose the situation.
2. Change the situation.
3. Change the perspective.

These strategies can be used independently or in combination with Stop and Slow
Down and Planful Problem Solving.

## Strategy One: Choose the Situation

Reduce unnecessary exposure to situations that predictably generate severe
stress or negative emotion.

This is not the claim that all stress should be avoided. The question is whether
some exposure is optional, excessive, or preventable.

Examples include:

- reducing time in reliably harmful environments;
- choosing different routines;
- limiting avoidable conflict;
- changing jobs or roles when feasible;
- selecting settings that reduce known triggers.

### Decision Question

> Can I reduce how often, how long, or under what conditions I enter this
> situation?

## Strategy Two: Change the Situation

When leaving is not feasible or desirable, modify the environment or conditions
that produce stress.

Examples include:

- clarifying expectations;
- changing workload;
- requesting resources;
- altering schedules;
- renegotiating responsibilities;
- improving the physical environment;
- setting boundaries;
- changing communication processes.

### Decision Question

> What part of this situation can be redesigned so it is less harmful or more
> manageable?

## Strategy Three: Change the Perspective

Change how the problem is interpreted without denying the facts.

Methods include:

- separating facts from assumptions;
- identifying positive or useful aspects;
- examining the problem from another viewpoint;
- using realistic coping statements;
- recalling previous successful coping;
- accepting that progress may take time;
- treating the challenge as a source of learning.

### Decision Question

> Is there a more accurate or useful way to understand this situation or my
> ability to cope with it?

## Combined Strategy Model

The three approaches are not mutually exclusive.

```text
Reduce exposure
      +
Modify conditions
      +
Change interpretation
      =
Broader emotion-regulation plan
```

A person may leave one harmful situation, redesign another, and practice a more
realistic perspective toward a third.

## Operational Decision Guide

| Question | Strategy |
|---|---|
| Can I reduce or avoid unnecessary exposure? | Choose the situation |
| Can I alter the environment, task, relationship, or conditions? | Change the situation |
| Can I interpret the facts or my coping ability more realistically? | Change the perspective |
| Am I too activated to decide? | Stop and Slow Down first |
| Is a structured plan needed? | Use Planful Problem Solving |

## Image Status

No source image is required. A later original three-path decision diagram may be
useful in the HTML edition.

# Optional Handout 4: Motivational Worksheet

## Purpose

The Motivational Worksheet is used when a person has identified a possible
action but remains ambivalent about beginning or following through.

It does not assume that resistance is irrational. Instead, it makes both sides
of the decision visible:

- the benefits and costs of **not moving forward**;
- the benefits and costs of **moving forward**.

This is a decisional-balance exercise. Its purpose is to replace a vague internal
struggle with an explicit comparison that can be inspected and discussed.

## Worksheet Structure

The worksheet begins with a brief problem statement and then uses two columns:

| Not moving forward | Moving forward |
|---|---|
| Benefits of staying where things are | Benefits of acting |
| Costs of staying where things are | Costs of acting |

The participant should include both immediate and delayed consequences. The goal
is not to force a preferred answer. It is to make the tradeoffs concrete enough
that a deliberate choice becomes possible.

## Example: Beginning Treatment

The treatment example shows common sources of ambivalence.

### Reasons not to begin

- avoiding effort;
- avoiding disappointment;
- preserving time;
- avoiding unfamiliar people or difficult discussion.

### Costs of not beginning

- conditions are likely to remain unchanged;
- no new coping skills are learned;
- the person may lose an opportunity for even partial improvement.

### Costs of beginning

- therapy requires time and effort;
- improvement is not guaranteed;
- the person may feel uncomfortable or disappointed.

### Potential benefits of beginning

- treatment creates a real possibility of change;
- even incomplete improvement may matter;
- useful skills may be learned even when symptoms do not disappear completely.

## Example: Following an Action Plan

The action-plan example concerns joining a book club to increase social
connection.

### Reasons not to act

- staying inside the comfort zone;
- avoiding expense;
- avoiding possible rejection or embarrassment;
- avoiding the time required to locate and evaluate a group.

### Costs of not acting

- social isolation remains unchanged;
- no new relationships are created;
- fewer opportunities arise for enjoyable reading and conversation.

### Costs of acting

- discomfort;
- expense;
- possible rejection;
- effort that may not produce the hoped-for result.

### Potential benefits of acting

- a chance to make friends;
- increased connection;
- exposure to new books and ideas;
- evidence that the person can take constructive action even under uncertainty.

## How to Use the Worksheet

1. Define one decision or blocked action.
2. List the benefits of not moving forward.
3. List the costs of not moving forward.
4. List the costs of moving forward.
5. List the benefits of moving forward.
6. Compare short-term relief with long-term consequences.
7. Decide whether a smaller first step would reduce the cost of action.
8. Use Planful Problem Solving to convert the decision into an action plan.

## Key Interpretation

Ambivalence usually means that **both options contain real costs**. The worksheet
does not treat hesitation as laziness or failure. It treats hesitation as a
decision system that has not yet been externalized.

# Measures

## Purpose of the Measures Section

The Measures section begins on packet page 78. These instruments are not teaching
chapters. They are structured assessments used to establish a baseline, monitor
change, identify areas of concern, and support clinical discussion.

This guide preserves:

- the name of each measure;
- the construct it appears to assess;
- its response structure;
- its relationship to PST-SP.

It does **not** present the measures as self-diagnostic tools or substitute for
clinical interpretation.

## 1. Suicide Cognitions Scale–Revised (SCS-R)

### Apparent Construct

The SCS-R assesses beliefs associated with suicidal thinking, including:

- perceived burdensomeness;
- hopelessness;
- inability to tolerate emotional pain;
- self-condemnation;
- beliefs that problems cannot be solved;
- perceived inability to cope;
- beliefs that help is unavailable.

### Format

- 16 statements;
- rated from **Strongly Disagree** to **Strongly Agree**;
- numeric values shown from 0 through 4;
- instructions ask the respondent to answer according to how they feel **right
  now**.

### Relationship to PST-SP

The scale maps closely to the program’s emphasis on:

- problem-solving beliefs;
- tolerability of distress;
- realistic optimism;
- perceived access to help;
- the distinction between a crisis state and an enduring truth.

## 2. Suicide-Related Coping Scale

### Apparent Construct

This scale evaluates perceived ability to respond to a suicidal crisis.

The photographed form includes items concerning:

- access to professional help;
- family or friends who can be contacted;
- knowledge of emergency facilities;
- safe places to go;
- distraction strategies;
- multiple coping options;
- willingness to confide in someone;
- help-seeking beliefs;
- limiting access to lethal means;
- ability to cope while alone;
- perceived helplessness;
- ability to switch strategies when one does not work.

### Format

- 17 statements across two pages;
- response range from **Strongly Disagree (0)** to **Strongly Agree (4)**;
- some items are positively stated and others negatively stated.

### Relationship to PST-SP

This measure reflects whether the participant has developed a **coping
repertoire**, not merely whether distress is present. It is closely related to:

- Safety Planning;
- Stop and Slow Down;
- externalizing support information;
- future forecasting;
- developing alternatives before crisis intensity peaks.

## 3. Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9)

### Apparent Construct

The PHQ-9 screens the frequency of common depressive symptoms over the previous
two weeks.

### Domains Shown

- reduced interest or pleasure;
- depressed mood or hopelessness;
- sleep disturbance;
- low energy;
- appetite changes;
- self-critical or failure-related thoughts;
- concentration difficulty;
- psychomotor slowing or agitation;
- thoughts of death or self-harm;
- functional difficulty.

### Format

Frequency is rated from:

- Not at all;
- Several days;
- More than half the days;
- Nearly every day.

A final item asks how difficult the reported problems have made work, home
responsibilities, or relationships.

### Relationship to PST-SP

The PHQ-9 measures symptom burden and functional effect. It is broader than the
problem-solving model itself but can help show whether changes in coping and
problem solving are accompanied by changes in depressive symptoms.

## 4. NPO-Q — Negative Problem Orientation Questionnaire

The photographed page identifies the measure as the **NPO-Q** by Robichaud and
Dugas (2005).

### Apparent Construct

The NPO-Q assesses negative orientation toward problems, including:

- seeing problems as threats;
- doubting one’s problem-solving capacity;
- expecting difficulty before trying;
- viewing problems as insurmountable;
- questioning personal ability;
- assuming problems cannot be solved;
- doubting that identified solutions will work.

### Format

The page uses a five-point scale:

1. Not at all true of me  
2. Slightly true  
3. Moderately true  
4. Often true  
5. Extremely true of me

### Relationship to PST-SP

This measure directly corresponds to the workbook’s distinction between:

- realistic optimism;
- pessimistic problem beliefs;
- planful problem solving;
- avoidant and impulsive styles.

It evaluates the cognitive stance that exists **before** the person begins the
technical problem-solving steps.

## Measures Interpretation Map

| Measure | Primary focus | PST-SP connection |
|---|---|---|
| SCS-R | Suicide-related beliefs and intolerability | Belief checking, hope, distress tolerance |
| Suicide-Related Coping Scale | Available coping responses and help-seeking | Safety planning, coping toolkit, future preparation |
| PHQ-9 | Depressive symptoms and functional burden | Symptom context and progress monitoring |
| NPO-Q | Negative beliefs about problems and ability | Problem orientation and realistic optimism |

## Caution About Use

These measures should be interpreted within their intended clinical context.
Scores can be affected by timing, crisis intensity, comprehension, and response
style. A number is not a complete description of the person, and a single
administration does not establish a permanent condition.
