## Adult Psychiatric Division, February 2024

My first assignment at the New Mexico Behavioral Health Institute was in the **Adult Psychiatric Division (APD)**. My first supervisor there established my original understanding of what a well-run housekeeping operation could look like.

She ran a demanding operation, but it was orderly rather than chaotic. The days moved quickly because we always knew what to do. The schedule, standards, communication, supervision, inventory, inspections, and paperwork all reinforced one another.

She did not depend on workers having exceptional attitudes or unusually high motivation. She created a system that produced consistent results from employees with different personalities, skill levels, and degrees of engagement.

Every worker followed a known schedule. We began and ended work in our assigned areas at approximately the same times each day and performed the same established routines.

The operational areas included:

- SPU, the Special Psychiatric Unit
- Choices
- Pinewood
- Tesuque 1
- Tesuque 2
- Dining room
- Common area
- Administrative hallway

The routine was demanding, but there was very little uncertainty. When one activity ended, the next activity was already known.

We did not repeatedly have to ask:

> Where should I be?
> What should I do next?
> Is this my responsibility?
> How much of this am I expected to complete?
> Which instruction takes priority?

We knew what we were doing, why we were doing it, when it was to be done, how it was to be performed, and what a completed result was supposed to look like.

That is what I mean when I say our days usually passed **without incident**. It did not mean nothing unusual happened. It meant no incident was allowed to leave us standing around wondering what to do.

Every morning also began with a brief huddle. The team received the usual announcements, learned about changes, and reviewed any immediate overrides before beginning work.

These meetings were not ceremonial. They were how she ensured that everyone began the day with the same information.

If something changed, she did not allow separate versions of the plan to develop among different workers. She made sure everyone remained on the same page.

## A continuous and unmistakable chain of command

The supervisor was either present herself or had a particularly qualified, capable, and friendly worker covering her responsibilities.

That counterpart remained in regular contact with the supervisor and could take over duties when necessary. The chain of command therefore never felt broken or uncertain.

Workers knew:

- who was directing the operation;
- who could answer a question;
- who could approve an exception;
- who was responsible for following up;
- how information would reach the supervisor.

## Uniform onboarding and one shared standard

The supervisor did not assume that a new employee already possessed the necessary skills.

Previous housekeeping experience did not exempt anyone from learning the APD method. Every worker received the same basic onboarding for the routines they would be expected to perform.

That created a shared operating standard rather than a collection of personal habits.

Everyone learned:

- the sequence of work;
- the expected method;
- the required level of detail;
- the timing of routines;
- proper equipment handling;
- closet organization;
- communication expectations;
- how to work in patient environment;
- what counted as complete.

Because everyone received the same training, correction remained connected to a known standard. It was not one worker’s opinion against another’s.

## Communication as part of the work

Communication was treated as an operational requirement, not merely a courtesy.

Workers did not ordinarily walk alone. Partners moved together.

When an exception was necessary, the employee leaving the pair had to communicate clearly:

- where they were going;
- why they were leaving;
- approximately when they expected to return.

The supervisor actively reinforced this rule.

If she encountered one worker alone, her first question was almost always:

> “Where is X?”

If the worker did not know, the absent partner could expect a friendly but definite scolding.

This rule supported several things at once:

- safety;
- awareness of employee locations;
- coordination;
- accountability;
- continuity of the assignment;
- respect between partners.

Nobody was supposed to disappear and leave the other employee wondering what had happened. Employee accounts of situations were always corroborated by each other's presence.

Her rounds combined several responsibilities:

- inspecting completed work;
- inspecting the general condition of spaces;
- checking compliance;
- checking whether workers were properly paired;
- observing whether routines were being followed;
- identifying supply shortages;
- restocking closets;
- following up on special instructions;
- verifying that operational changes were producing the intended result.

She did not announce a change and then assume that it had worked.

If she introduced a new emphasis or corrected a procedure, you could count on her returning almost like clockwork to see whether the desired outcome was actually being produced.

Her management cycle was complete:

> instruction → work → inspection → feedback → correction or confirmation

## Predictable, nearly white-glove inspections

Her inspections were demanding and extremely consistent.

If crumbs were left on the dining-room floor, you knew she would find them.

If even a small dribble remained on a toilet seat, she would notice it.

If dusty windowsills had already been discussed, she would notice whether you remembered them without being reminded again.

She paid attention to the same details every time. Her inspections were almost as predictable as a train schedule.

That predictability mattered. Workers did not have to wonder whether a detail would suddenly matter one day and be ignored the next. The standard did not shift according to her mood or according to which employee had performed the work.

Over time, her attention trained our attention. We began to see the room through the anticipated inspection before she arrived.

## Specific praise as well as specific correction

Because she paid close attention, she also noticed when the details were done correctly.

If a worker remembered the windowsills, improved a routine, left an area in excellent condition, or corrected a previous weakness, she would acknowledge the exact thing that had been done well.

Her praise was not vague or pro forma. She did not appear to invent compliments merely because supervisors are sometimes told to praise employees.

She could identify:

- what was done correctly;
- where it was done;
- why it mattered;
- how it reflected improvement.

That made the praise credible.

Workers knew that mistakes were visible, but good work was visible too.

## Friendly correction without loss of authority

She often delivered corrections in a friendly, almost humorous way.

The tone could be light, but the expectation remained firm. Nobody misunderstood the humor as permission to ignore the standard.

She was someone workers took seriously, but she was not mean.

She could communicate:

> “You know better than this.”

without humiliating the employee or turning the interaction into a personal conflict.

She remained professional. She did not yell theatrically, become emotionally erratic, or use correction to display power.

## Neutrality and professional inscrutability

She listened to her workers and made herself available for individual conversations. A worker could receive a one-on-one meeting regularly or at a moment’s notice when something needed to be discussed.

At the same time, she showed no obvious favoritism.

She was friendly but professionally inscrutable. Workers could not easily manipulate her through closeness, flattery, complaints, cliques, or emotional pressure.

Her standards remained larger than her personal relationships.

That helped employees trust that:

- the same work would be inspected;
- the same expectations would apply;
- praise would be earned;
- correction would be connected to performance;
- personal preference would not redefine the job.

The housekeeping closets in the different units were expected to contain the same inventory, arranged in the same way, with known quantities of each item on hand.

## Paperwork connected to physical reality

APD used the same general kind of paper daily sheets later used at Meadows.

The difference was not primarily the form. The difference was how seriously she used it.

She:

- reviewed the daily sheets;
- audited them;
- dated the audits;
- compared recorded work with actual room conditions;
- followed up when the record and the physical space did not agree.

A checked box was not treated as an empty administrative gesture. It represented a claim that the work had been completed.

If a bathroom was marked complete and she found a dribble on the toilet seat, the physical condition contradicted the recorded completion.

Her audits gave the paperwork deeper operational meaning.

The paper still had its full suite of shortcomings:

- difficult to search;
- difficult to aggregate;
- difficult to analyze over time;
- difficult to share;
- difficult to preserve as an accessible history;
- dependent on physical handling;
- unable to automate follow-up.

## Uniform results from different workers

Perhaps the strongest evidence of her effectiveness was that she produced relatively uniform results from workers with very different levels of engagement and different attitudes toward the job.

Some employees were highly motivated.

Others were less invested, more difficult, tired, indifferent, or simply interested in completing the shift.

The operation did not require every worker to become personally enthusiastic.

The system still produced consistent results because:

- everyone received the same training;
- the schedule was known;
- routines were standardized;
- supplies were controlled;
- closets had a known expected state;
- workers knew what would be inspected;
- communication was required;
- the supervisor followed up;
- incomplete work was found;
- good work was recognized;
- performance feedback was continuous.

A worker’s attitude might affect how pleasantly the person moved through the day, but it did not substantially change the definition of acceptable work.

She reduced variation in results without trying to make every employee the same kind of person.

## The lesson she established for me

She was my first supervisor at NMBHI, so she became my original reference point for housekeeping supervision.

She showed me that a housekeeping department could be:

- demanding without being chaotic;
- exact without being cruel;
- disciplined without being hostile;
- friendly without becoming unprofessional;
- predictable without becoming passive;
- highly accountable without depending on fear.

She also showed that workplace harmony can be an outcome of operational clarity.

When workers know exactly what to do, why to do it, when to do it, how to do it, and what completed work looks like, many interpersonal conflicts never develop.

She did not rely on harmony first and hope that good work would follow.

She created the conditions for good work, and harmony followed.

That makes APD the essential beginning of the later Meadows story. I did not arrive at Meadows without a standard for comparison. I had already experienced a system in which supervision, communication, training, inventory, inspections, paperwork, and employee relationships worked together—and produced dependable results regardless of individual attitude.
