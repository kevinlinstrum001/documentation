# Navy Recruitment, Fraudulent Diploma, and Recruiting Investigation

## Personal Historical Narrative

**Document type:** Personal recollection / historical narrative  
**Status:** Detailed working account  
**Primary subject:** Navy recruiting, Nuclear Field screening, fraudulent education documentation, and subsequent recruiting investigation in Ohio  
**Author perspective:** First-person recollection  

> **Record note:** This document records my present recollection of events that occurred during my Navy recruiting process. Some dates, names, titles, and exact administrative terminology may need later verification against Navy records. Where I am uncertain, I have tried to preserve that uncertainty rather than convert memory into a false precision.

---

## 1. Overview

My entry into the Navy was preceded by a recruiting problem that became much larger and stranger than it ever needed to be. The original problem was not that I tried to falsify my education or that I was caught trying to obtain something I was not qualified for. The problem began because my recruiter was in a hurry to get me processed before I became too old for the Nuclear Field program, or at least before he believed the age issue would become difficult to manage.

I was getting close to age twenty-four. My recruiter told me that after twenty-four I would not be able to get into the program. I did not know the precise regulation at the time, and I relied heavily on what recruiters told me about the process. In retrospect, I do not think the situation was as absolute as he represented it. The Navy had waiver processes for many issues, and during my own accession I completed a large amount of supplemental paperwork, explanations, and what I then thought of generally as “waivers.” Many of those documents were connected with the SF-86 and the security-background process.

The recruiter apparently believed that my educational history was going to slow or block the process. Rather than resolving the education issue through the proper administrative route, he created a false diploma for me.

I told him that I did not think we needed to do that. I believed I would score very well on the Navy's testing and that my aptitude would speak for itself. He told me to trust him and said, in substance, that he did this sort of thing all the time.

That decision created the entire later problem.

---

## 2. Educational Background and the Recruiter's Sense of Urgency

My education did not fit neatly into a conventional high-school record. I had not followed a normal uninterrupted path through high school, and I had not taken much formal mathematics in school. That background generated additional accession and security paperwork.

At the time, I had also lived in many different places. The SF-86 process required me to account for my history in considerable detail. I remember having to write numerous statements and explanations. At the time I referred to many of these documents collectively as “waivers,” although some were probably supplemental statements, security-processing explanations, or other accession documents rather than waivers in the strict administrative sense.

My recruiter focused heavily on my age. I was approaching twenty-four, and he acted as though there was very little time left to get me into the Navy and especially into the Nuclear Field pipeline. Whether he misunderstood the exact rule, exaggerated it to create urgency, or simply did not want to deal with a longer waiver process, I cannot say with certainty.

What I do know is that his haste was central to the problem. Instead of taking the time to resolve my educational record correctly, he created a diploma that was not legitimate.

---

## 3. The False Diploma

I did not ask the recruiter to make a diploma for me.

I did not believe that we needed one.

I told him that I expected to do very well on the ASVAB and that I did not think falsifying a diploma was necessary or appropriate. He did not accept my judgment about my likely test performance. He told me, essentially, to trust him because he knew the recruiting process and did this sort of thing regularly.

I was not yet in the Navy, and this is important to how I remember the situation. I was an applicant dealing with uniformed Navy personnel who picked me up, drove me to offices, gave me instructions, and conducted what felt like official military business. The boundary between “this is a request from a recruiter” and “this is an order I am required to obey” was not clear to me at the time.

The recruiter was the institutional authority in the relationship. He was the person who supposedly knew the rules. I was the applicant.

Nevertheless, I remember specifically telling him that I did not think the false diploma was necessary.

---

## 4. The ASVAB Result

The situation changed dramatically after I took the ASVAB.

I received a score of **98**. I remember the score as a 98 on my ASVAB; in later technical terminology this may correspond to the AFQT percentile associated with the ASVAB battery, but the important fact for this narrative is the number I was given and remember: **98**.

The recruiter became very excited about the result. A score that high was valuable in recruiting terms. Recruiters worked under production goals and incentive systems in which some accessions were worth more than others. High-scoring recruits were especially valuable, and recruits who could qualify for difficult programs such as the Nuclear Field were even more desirable.

My score quickly became something that recruiters talked about. I later understood that a high-quality accession could contribute more heavily toward a recruiter's production goals than an ordinary contract. Other characteristics and hard-to-fill programs could also increase the recruiting value of a contract.

This is important because it explains why other recruiters already knew about me and my test score before the later investigation was fully underway.

---

## 5. Nuclear Field Qualification

After the ASVAB, the Nuclear Field became a realistic option rather than a hypothetical one.

I also took the **Nuclear Field Qualifying Test (NFQT)** and remember scoring **56**. I believe I would have done better with more formal mathematics education, because mathematics was an area in which my schooling had been limited. Even so, my scores were sufficient to make Nuclear Field processing a serious possibility.

At this point the recruiter's earlier shortcut became a major liability.

He told me that if I continued toward the Nuclear Field, the Navy would conduct a more serious security-background investigation and that the false diploma could be discovered. He began putting pressure on me to give up the Nuclear Field option.

This frightened me because the problem had reversed itself. Before testing, the recruiter had decided that a fake diploma was supposedly necessary to get me into the Navy quickly. After I proved that I had a very high aptitude score and could actually pursue the Nuclear Field, I was being told that I should abandon that opportunity because the false document he had created might be exposed by the background investigation.

I did not think I should have to reorganize my career choices in order to conceal something I had not wanted done in the first place.

My reaction was increasingly simple: **I wanted out.**

I did not want to continue an enlistment if continuing required me to participate in or conceal fraudulent paperwork.

---

## 6. The Recruiter's Phone Call to Another Recruiter

The strangest and most important event in the whole story was initiated by the recruiter himself.

He handed me his cell phone and told me to speak with another Navy recruiter somewhere else in Ohio. His idea was that I could explain the situation without giving my name and that this other recruiter would confirm that what he was telling me was normal or acceptable.

I thought this was a strange thing to do, but I followed his suggestion.

I spoke to the other recruiter anonymously. I explained the situation as I understood it. I told him what had happened with the education documentation, what I wanted to do, and why I was uncomfortable continuing.

Instead of validating my recruiter's conduct, the other recruiter agreed with me.

He told me, in substance, that I should do what I believed was right and that if I wanted to leave the program rather than proceed under a fraudulent enlistment, I should leave. He did **not** tell me that I should go along with the false document.

The call therefore produced exactly the opposite result from what my recruiter apparently expected.

More importantly, the other recruiter recognized what was happening.

He contacted the recruiting organization and reported the matter.

That is how the incident came to official attention.

---

## 7. I Did Not Turn the Recruiter In

This point is central to my memory of the entire affair.

I did **not** report my recruiter.

I did not call a command, inspector, investigator, or another recruiter to accuse him.

I did not create the false diploma.

I did not ask him to create it.

I told him I did not think it was necessary.

I did not initiate the phone call that exposed the problem. **He handed me the phone himself.**

The other recruiter independently concluded that something improper was happening and independently reported it.

By the time the Navy began questioning me, the matter was already their problem.

I viewed myself as someone who had been caught in the middle of misconduct by Navy recruiting personnel, not as the person who had initiated an accusation against the recruiter.

---

## 8. Why the Navy Knew Who the Recruiter Was

Although I did not identify the recruiter during the later investigation, I do not believe it was difficult for the Navy to determine who he was.

My score had already become known among recruiters. The recruiter had talked about my **98**, and a number of recruiters knew about the high-scoring applicant and the possibility of a Nuclear Field contract.

The other recruiter had also received the unusual phone call from the recruiter's own cell phone or through a connection made by him. The combination of the story, the timing, the unusually high ASVAB score, and the recruiting network made the responsible recruiter identifiable without my cooperation.

In other words, the trail did not depend upon me naming him.

The recruiter had effectively created his own evidentiary trail by discussing my score and by personally arranging the call with the other recruiter.

---

## 9. The Investigation

The matter became a long formal investigation that lasted approximately **ten months**, as I remember it.

I found the process exhausting and increasingly absurd.

I was repeatedly called in and questioned for long periods. The interviews often seemed to cover the same questions again and again. I remember being taken to offices and dealing with Navy legal officers rather than simply chiefs or ordinary recruiting personnel.

The people questioning me were, as I remember them, **JAG officers** or officers performing a legal/investigative function.

One officer I remember as **Lieutenant Ulrich** or a similar name. I am not completely certain of the spelling and would want to verify it from records before treating the name as exact.

The repeated questioning was particularly frustrating because I had already decided that I did not want to participate in a fraudulent enlistment. From my point of view, the Navy had its own employee problem to investigate. I did not understand why I was expected to spend month after month helping them prove what their recruiter had done.

---

## 10. The Communist Questions

One detail from the interviews has remained especially vivid because it seemed so archaic.

I was repeatedly asked questions about whether I was a communist or had associations with communists or similar organizations.

By the time I had been through the interview process several times, I knew the questions were coming.

During one interview with Lieutenant Ulrich, I stopped her near the beginning and said, in substance:

> I have never in my life met a communist, and I do not know anybody who has ever known one, so you probably do not need to ask me all of these questions again.

She insisted on asking them anyway.

At the time I remember thinking: **When were these questions written?**

The questions felt like they belonged to another era, but the officer treated them as mandatory. In retrospect, I understand that she may have been required to complete a standardized security or suitability questionnaire and therefore could not simply skip sections because both of us already knew what my answers would be.

That did not make the experience feel any less ridiculous after being questioned repeatedly for months.

---

## 11. Refusal to Identify the Recruiter

Throughout the investigation, the Navy wanted me to identify or confirm the recruiter responsible for the false diploma.

I refused.

My reasoning was personal rather than procedural.

The recruiter was a petty officer and, as I remember it, a relatively new father with a baby at home. I knew that if he was going to get into serious trouble, that trouble could affect his career and his family.

I did not believe it should be my job to destroy his career in order to save my own enlistment opportunity.

My attitude was essentially this:

- If I got in trouble, I was not even in the Navy yet.
- I could walk away.
- The Navy employed the recruiter and supervised its recruiting organization.
- Another recruiter had already discovered and reported the misconduct.
- If the Navy wanted to prove which of its own people had done it, the Navy could investigate its own people.

I was willing to accept consequences to myself rather than become the person who formally identified him.

I did not consider that refusal evidence that I supported what he had done. I had objected to the false diploma from the beginning and later wanted to leave precisely because I did not want to proceed under fraudulent circumstances.

To me, there was a moral distinction between refusing to participate in the fraud and agreeing to become the government's witness against the person who had created it.

The Navy did not necessarily view that distinction the same way.

---

## 12. Moral Disqualification

My recollection is that I was eventually considered **morally disqualified**, particularly in connection with the Nuclear Field process.

From the Navy's point of view, my refusal to cooperate fully with the investigation may have been interpreted as a character, truthfulness, or suitability problem.

From my point of view, I knew exactly why I was refusing: I would not falsely accept responsibility for the recruiter’s decision, and I would not identify him simply to improve my own position after the Navy already had independent information about what had happened.

I understood that the Navy might judge me negatively for that decision.

My feeling then, and my recollection now, was essentially: **If that makes them think I am a bad person, they can think that. I know why I made the decision.**

---

## 13. The Ohio Recruiting Signs and Rule Change

After the incident, I was told by the recruiter who later took over my case that the recruiting organization in **Ohio** had changed a rule or procedure and that recruiting stations were required to display new warning signs concerning false information or fraudulent enlistment.

I do **not** claim that my case created the federal offense of fraudulent enlistment. That law and military offense existed long before my enlistment attempt.

What I remember is much narrower and more specific:

- the signs were not present before my case;
- after the case, recruiters in Ohio were required to post new warnings;
- the recruiter who subsequently handled my case told me about the change;
- he knew what had happened because the recruiting personnel themselves had discussed the matter.

I remember the signs as saying something roughly equivalent to **do not lie**, **do not provide false information**, or **do not participate in fraudulent enlistment**. I do not remember the exact wording.

My present interpretation is that the Ohio recruiting command may have adopted a new local or regional internal-control measure after the investigation. Such a sign would create an independent warning to applicants so that a recruiter could not privately tell an applicant that falsifying information was normal while the applicant remained unaware of official Navy policy.

This interpretation should be verified against historical recruiting directives if records can be located.

---

## 14. Recruiting Incentives and Why the 98 Mattered

Another important part of the story is the recruiting incentive system.

Recruiters did not treat every contract as identical. High-quality recruits and difficult-to-fill programs could be especially valuable toward recruiting goals. I remember recruiters talking about point values and desirable combinations of characteristics.

A high ASVAB score was one such valuable factor. Nuclear Field qualification could make a contract substantially more desirable. The recruiter therefore had a strong professional incentive to get a high-scoring applicant successfully contracted.

When I initially told him that a fake diploma was unnecessary because I would score very well, he did not believe me.

Then I scored **98**.

That result made the situation more ironic. The applicant for whom he had taken an improper shortcut turned out to be an unusually high-scoring applicant who could plausibly qualify for one of the Navy's most demanding enlisted technical pipelines.

The false diploma did not solve a problem. It created a much larger one.

---

## 15. The Central Contradiction

The whole affair can be reduced to a contradiction:

1. The recruiter was afraid that my educational history and age would make the process difficult.
2. He created a false diploma to make the process easier and faster.
3. I objected and told him I expected to test well enough that this should not be necessary.
4. I scored 98 and became a strong candidate for Nuclear Field processing.
5. The recruiter then became afraid that Nuclear Field security scrutiny would expose the false diploma.
6. He pressured me to give up the very opportunity my scores had earned.
7. I decided I would rather leave than continue under fraudulent circumstances.
8. The recruiter himself connected me with another recruiter to obtain reassurance.
9. The other recruiter instead reported the misconduct.
10. The Navy then spent approximately ten months investigating the matter and repeatedly asking me to identify the recruiter.
11. I refused to identify him even though the Navy could apparently determine his identity independently.

The recruiter’s attempt to save time ultimately produced the longest possible delay.

---

## 16. What I Believe My Own Role Was

I think it is important to state my role precisely because a simplified version of this story can make it sound as though I participated in a fraudulent enlistment scheme and later refused to cooperate after being caught.

That is not how I remember the event.

My role was:

- I was the applicant.
- I had an unconventional educational history that required additional processing.
- I told the recruiter that I did not think a false diploma was necessary.
- The recruiter created the document anyway and represented the practice as something he routinely did.
- I scored 98 on the ASVAB and 56 on the NFQT.
- I became increasingly concerned when the recruiter told me that Nuclear Field security screening could expose the false document.
- I wanted to withdraw rather than continue under fraudulent circumstances.
- The recruiter himself connected me to the other recruiter.
- I truthfully described the situation during that phone call.
- The other recruiter independently reported it.
- During the later investigation I refused to identify the original recruiter.

I accept that investigators could regard my refusal to name him as noncooperation. But noncooperation with the later investigation is not the same thing as originating the fraudulent document or initiating the misconduct.

---

## 17. Why the Experience Stayed With Me

The experience remained memorable because it was one of my first encounters with the strange boundary between military authority and civilian status.

Recruiters wore uniforms. They picked me up in government-associated transportation or their vehicles, took me to offices, told me where to go, and placed me in front of officers who questioned me for hours. Everything about the environment communicated command authority.

But I was still in the accession process, and for much of it I did not understand clearly when I was legally required to comply, when I was merely being asked to cooperate, and when I could simply say that I wanted to leave.

That ambiguity mattered. If I had understood the boundary more clearly, I might have said much earlier that I wanted nothing further to do with the entire process.

Instead, an incident that began with one recruiter's shortcut became a ten-month legal and administrative ordeal.

---

## 18. Later Outcome

Despite the recruiting debacle, I ultimately did enter the Navy and served as an **Electronics Technician (ET)** rather than entering the Nuclear Field pipeline.

My later Navy service therefore followed a very different route from the one that appeared possible immediately after the 98 ASVAB score and NFQT qualification.

The recruiting incident remains important to my personal history because it occurred at the point where my civilian life and Navy career intersected. It also illustrates how organizational incentives, administrative shortcuts, ambiguous authority, and a single false document can produce consequences far beyond the original issue.

## 20. Condensed Chronology

**Approaching age 24**  
Recruiter believes Nuclear Field age limits create urgency and wants to move the accession quickly.

**Education issue arises**  
Recruiter decides to create a false diploma. I tell him I do not think this is necessary and expect to test well. He tells me to trust him and indicates that he has done this before.

**ASVAB testing**  
I receive a score of **98**, making me an unusually high-scoring applicant.

**NFQT testing**  
I receive a score of **56**, making Nuclear Field processing a realistic option despite my limited formal mathematics education.

**Security concern develops**  
Recruiter tells me that Nuclear Field security/background scrutiny could expose the false diploma and begins pressuring me to abandon the Nuclear Field option.

**I decide I want out**  
I do not want to continue if doing so requires concealing or participating in fraudulent documentation.

**Recruiter initiates outside call**  
The recruiter hands me his cell phone and has me anonymously explain the situation to another Ohio recruiter, expecting that recruiter to validate his advice.

**Second recruiter disagrees**  
The other recruiter tells me that I should not proceed with a fraudulent enlistment and that leaving is a legitimate choice.

**Second recruiter reports the matter**  
Without me initiating a complaint, the other recruiter reports the incident to the recruiting organization.

**Investigation begins**  
The Navy begins a lengthy recruiting/legal investigation. Because recruiters already know about the applicant who scored 98 and because the original recruiter initiated the telephone contact, his identity is apparently not difficult to determine.

**Repeated JAG interviews**  
For approximately ten months I am repeatedly questioned, including standardized security/suitability questions. I remember Lieutenant Ulrich or a similarly named officer repeatedly asking questions including whether I was or had been associated with communists.

**I refuse to identify the recruiter**  
I do not want to be the person who destroys the recruiter's career or harms his new family. I regard identifying and disciplining Navy personnel as the Navy's responsibility, particularly because another recruiter has already reported the misconduct.

**Moral disqualification**  
My refusal to cooperate fully is apparently treated as a moral/suitability problem and affects my Nuclear Field prospects.

**Ohio warning-sign change**  
A later recruiter tells me that Ohio recruiting stations are now required to display new warnings concerning lying or fraudulent enlistment. I understand this as a new Ohio recruiting procedure following the incident, not as the creation of the underlying federal offense.

**Later Navy service**  
I ultimately enter the Navy and serve as an Electronics Technician rather than a Nuclear Field ET.

---

## 21. Closing Reflection

The most important thing I want preserved about this event is that I did not set out to deceive the Navy and I did not initiate the action that exposed the recruiter.

I told the recruiter the false diploma was unnecessary. He made it. I later became frightened precisely because I realized the document could compromise a legitimate opportunity that my own test scores had earned. When I decided I wanted to leave rather than continue under fraudulent circumstances, the recruiter tried to reassure me by connecting me directly to another recruiter. That recruiter recognized the misconduct and reported it.

Everything after that—the investigation, the repeated questioning, the pressure to identify the recruiter, the moral disqualification, and the later changes I was told had been made in Ohio recruiting—grew out of a decision I had opposed at the beginning.

The experience taught me very early that an organization can create a problem through its own personnel and then place enormous pressure on the individual caught inside the process to help the organization reconstruct what its own system allowed to happen.
